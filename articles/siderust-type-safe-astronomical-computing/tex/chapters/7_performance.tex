
\section{Experimental Method}
\label{sec:evaluation}

Paired numerical reductions evaluate transformations, ephemerides and observation workflows under
specified inputs and conventions. Compiler and runtime controls separately test representation
compatibility, value-dependent validity and data coverage. Same-model comparisons assess
implementation residuals; analytical ephemeris comparisons assess differences between physical
models after frame alignment.

\subsection{Experimental setup and validation strategy}

All experiments use the published Siderust release \cite{Valles_Puig_Siderust_Type-Safe_Astronomy},
with the corresponding dependency graph fixed by the committed Cargo lockfile. The evaluated
software revision, compiler environment, dependency versions and reproduction commands are
documented in \ref{sec:availability}.

Independent numerical references use ERFA through PyERFA and CSPICE through SpiceyPy. ERFA provides
reference implementations for astronomical time, Earth rotation and coordinate transformations,
while CSPICE supplies ephemeris and planetary-orientation calculations. Additional analytical
geometry calculations provide references for surface models and selected boundary cases.

The experiments distinguish three forms of verification. Direct numerical comparisons measure
residuals between implementations evaluating the same observable. Boundary and runtime controls
examine value-dependent conditions, including invalid geometry and unavailable data. Compiler
controls test whether incompatible scientific representations are rejected before execution. The
outcomes of these complementary checks are presented in Section~8.

Numerical comparisons use observable-specific residuals. Position differences are measured by the
Euclidean norm

\begin{equation}
    \Delta r =
    \left\|
    \boldsymbol r_{\mathrm{Siderust}}
    -\boldsymbol r_{\mathrm{reference}}
    \right\|_2.
\end{equation}

Rotation matrices are compared using the maximum absolute element difference. Time and altitude
residuals are absolute differences, while azimuth differences use the smaller wrapped angular
separation.

Acceptance limits are selected according to the numerical representation and scale of each
observable. They account for floating-point evaluation and differences between equivalent
implementations; they do not represent physical uncertainty budgets. All experimental inputs,
reference implementations, numerical records, tolerances and reproduction instructions accompany
the manuscript at \url{https://github.com/Siderust/docs}.

\subsection{Astronomical transformations and ephemerides}
\label{sec:ephemeris-method}

The first group of experiments evaluates astronomical time, reference-system transformations and
planetary ephemerides. The objective is to assess numerical agreement while controlling the
conventions and data that determine the meaning of each computed quantity.

\paragraph{Time and reference-system transformations}

Earth rotation angle, IAU 2006 Greenwich mean sidereal time, mean obliquity and the IAU 2006/2000A
bias--precession--nutation matrix are evaluated at 1,000 Julian dates,

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{JD}_i =
    2451545-36525+73.125i,
    \qquad 0\leq i<1000.
\end{equation}

Equal numerical TT and UT1 dates are prescribed as controlled inputs for these transformation
tests, isolating their numerical implementations from external Earth-orientation data.

Time-scale conversion is examined separately using 13 civil UTC labels covering the beginning of
the modern UTC regime, selected dates around J2000, the leap seconds introduced in 1972, 2015 and
2016, and a contemporary epoch. Each selected leap-second transition includes the preceding second,
the inserted second and the following midnight.

The comparisons use Tempoch's bundled UTC--TAI data, whose source and checksum are recorded in the
reproduction materials. ERFA two-part UTC representations are converted to uniform TAI and TT
before differences are evaluated.

Both conventional full-Julian-date outputs and split-second representations are examined. The
latter retain the precision of the underlying two-part epochs, with residuals evaluated using
50-digit decimal arithmetic. This distinction is important because a single floating-point Julian
date near J2000 has a spacing of approximately $40\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, substantially coarser than a
split representation.

No measured Earth-orientation dataset is used in these controlled time and rotation tests.

\paragraph{Ephemeris comparisons}

Earth heliocentric and Moon geocentric positions are evaluated using the DE440s planetary ephemeris
\cite{park2021de440,naifspk} at 1,000 TT epochs,

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{JD}_i = 2451545+8i,
    \qquad 0\leq i<1000.
\end{equation}

The independent CSPICE reduction converts TT to ephemeris time using \texttt{naif0012.tls} and
evaluates the same kernel. Comparing the same ephemeris data isolates the implementation of kernel
evaluation, time handling, coordinate transformations and center relationships from differences
between physical ephemeris models.

Reference-frame alignment is essential for these comparisons. Siderust exposes a canonical IAU 2006
mean-ecliptic J2000 representation for supported analytical and kernel-backed ephemerides. CSPICE
ICRF coordinates are transformed into this representation using ERFA \texttt{ecm06(2451545,0)}. The
distinct SPICE \texttt{ECLIPJ2000} frame is not substituted.

Runtime kernel evaluation also exercises coverage and metadata constraints. Supported SPK Type 2/3
segments are checked for the required frame metadata, valid coverage and supported center
relationships. Coverage probes include declared TDB endpoints, interior points one second from each
boundary and exterior points one day beyond coverage.

These metadata checks establish supported representations and query conditions. Their physical
interpretation additionally depends on the documented frame realization and provenance of the
supplied kernel. For the DE440s comparison, the ICRF realization is established from the ephemeris
documentation rather than inferred solely from its frame ID.

\paragraph{Analytical ephemeris normalization}

A separate comparison evaluates the analytical VSOP87A and ELP2000-82B ephemerides against DE440s.
Unlike the same-kernel comparison, the residuals here also reflect differences between astronomical
theories.

The analytical and kernel-backed calculations use the same target bodies, coordinate centers, 1,000
TT epochs and geometric correction policy. Their native reference-frame realizations are explicitly
mapped into the canonical Siderust representation.

For column vectors, the transformations are

\begin{align}
    C & = R_x(-\epsilon_0)B_0,
    \qquad \epsilon_0=84381.406'',                 \\
    \boldsymbol r_{\mathrm{VSOP,pub}}
      & = CHV\boldsymbol r_{\mathrm{VSOP,native}}, \\
    \boldsymbol r_{\mathrm{ELP,pub}}
      & = CHL\boldsymbol r_{\mathrm{ELP,Laskar}},  \\
    \boldsymbol r_{\mathrm{SPK,pub}}
      & = C\boldsymbol r_{\mathrm{ICRF}}.
\end{align}

Here $B_0$ denotes the J2000 bias matrix from ERFA \texttt{bp06}, so $C$ corresponds to
\texttt{ecm06(2451545,0)}. The matrix $H$ provides the J2000 FK5-to-ICRS orientation, $V$ is the
native VSOP-to-FK5 transformation, and $L$ is the corresponding ELP82B-to-FK5 realization tie.

The ELP Laskar rotation is applied before the native-frame correction, without duplicating the
transformation. Both analytical theories evaluate their required time arguments in TDB. The same
fixed spatial mapping is applied to VSOP velocities.

The implementation and native-frame conventions are documented in the versioned software references
\cite{siderustFrameImplementation,siderustFrames}.

\subsection{Integrated observation workflows}
\label{sec:observation-method}

The independent reference calculations reproduce the complete observing geometry of the
applications in Section~6.

\paragraph{Terrestrial altitude-event search}

The Antares application is evaluated on 1896 July 14 in the proleptic Gregorian calendar. The
observing day is defined in UT1 and sampled at five-minute intervals, including both endpoints.

Because the observation predates modern UTC, the required TT epochs are obtained from a historical
model of

\begin{equation}
    \Delta T = \mathrm{TT}-\mathrm{UT1}.
\end{equation}

The adopted Tempoch model \cite{tempochHistoricalTime} uses three-point interpolation of a biennial
historical $\Delta T$ table. Its source data, interpolation prescription, selected indices and
checksums are preserved with the experimental records.

For the selected date, the interpolated $\Delta T$ varies from $\ViewingDeltaTStart$ to
$\ViewingDeltaTEnd$ s over the day. The model's documented conservative historical accuracy
allowance is approximately $\pm15$ s, rather than a date-specific statistical uncertainty.

The independent reference reconstructs UT1 epochs from ERFA calendar conversions and derives TT
using the same prescribed historical time model. Neither calculation extrapolates modern UTC or
measured Earth-orientation parameters into the nineteenth century.

The stellar target uses the fixed ICRS catalog direction defined in
Section~\ref{sec:antares-example}. Proper motion, stellar parallax, aberration and atmospheric
refraction are omitted consistently in both implementations.

The reference applies ERFA \texttt{pnm06a} for bias, precession and nutation, followed by sidereal
rotation and \texttt{hd2ae} for horizontal projection. The Siderust calculation is compared using
equivalent reference-system conventions.

Altitude and wrapped-azimuth acceptance limits are $3\times10^{-11}$ and $10^{-10}$ degrees,
respectively. An additional comparison with ERFA \texttt{gst06a} examines the effect of the
equivalent sidereal-time formulation.

A common altitude function supplies Siderust's threshold-crossing and culmination operations on a
TT search axis. The adapter converts each trial epoch to UT1 with the same historical model; event
times are reported in UT1. Independent ERFA-based bisection and extremum searches provide reference
event times, separately from the sampling grid.

The event search uses a numerical time tolerance of \ViewingEventTolerance{} s. The complete
sampled altitude series, selected intervals, five-minute crossing brackets and refined event times
are recorded and cross-checked against the corresponding figure.

The historical $\Delta T$ uncertainty affects the physical interpretation of the resulting event
times. It is distinct from the numerical agreement between implementations evaluating the same
prescribed model.

\paragraph{Earth observation from the lunar surface}

The lunar application combines an ephemeris-derived finite target position, the Moon's analytical
orientation and the observer's surface geometry.

The selected TT epoch is constructed using separate calendar-day and within-day time components.
Both the ephemeris calculation and the analytical lunar orientation use the documented seven-term
TT-to-TDB approximation described in USNO Circular 179 \cite{kaplanCircular179}.

The independent Python reference evaluates the same time prescription from the specified TT epoch.
CSPICE then evaluates DE440s and the analytical \texttt{IAU\_MOON} orientation at the corresponding
TDB argument.

The high-level runtime ephemeris path converts TT through a full Julian date. To preserve
input-epoch resolution, the application instead calls the public \texttt{SpkKernel} evaluator with
explicit TDB seconds and checks segment frame, center, type and coverage metadata. The resulting
selenocentric position is represented through the typed coordinate interface, retaining its center,
frame and length unit.

The independent reduction constructs the full geometric observation using CSPICE ephemerides and
rotation matrices together with separately implemented observer subtraction and local-horizon
projection.

Comparison includes the inertial target vector, body-fixed rotation, observer position, local
coordinates, altitude, azimuth and range. The principal acceptance limits are $3\times10^{-9}$
degrees for angles and $2\times10^{-8}$ km for range.

The calculation additionally compares the surface-observer altitude with the corresponding
Moon-center direction, isolating the contribution of finite-distance observer parallax.

A separate diagnostic uses CSPICE's native \texttt{unitim} TT-to-TDB conversion in place of the
shared seven-term prescription. Its residuals are reported separately from the matched-convention
comparison, using the original acceptance bounds.

\subsection{Surface geometry and boundary cases}

Additional experiments test body-fixed coordinates, local basis construction, observer displacement
and finite-distance geometry, including geometric boundaries.

Lunar references use CSPICE \texttt{IAU\_MOON} rotations with \texttt{pck00011.tpc} and
\texttt{naif0012.tls}, combined with independently constructed surface positions and local tangent
bases.

Martian geometry uses an oblate reference ellipsoid with semiaxes

\begin{equation}
    a=3\,396\,190\ \mathrm{m},
    \qquad
    b=3\,376\,200\ \mathrm{m},
\end{equation}

consistent with the NAIF \texttt{BODY499\_RADII} values \cite{naifpck00011}.

Independent ellipsoid intersections and surface-normal calculations provide references for
planetocentric and planetographic latitude conventions.

The primary lunar comparisons use acceptance limits of $10^{-10}$ per rotation-matrix element,
$2\times10^{-8}$ degrees for angles, $10^{-3}$ m for range and $0.1$ m for Cartesian components.

The analytical boundary suite applies a tighter $2\times10^{-6}$ m lunar range limit and the same
$2\times10^{-8}$-degree angular limit where the angle is defined.

For Mars, position and local-basis bounds are $10^{-8}$ m and $10^{-14}$, respectively.

Different observables require different numerical scales. For a finite target, a rotation
perturbation $\delta R$ contributes a Cartesian displacement of order

\begin{equation}
    \|\delta R\|\,\|\boldsymbol r\|.
\end{equation}

A small angular or matrix residual can therefore produce a larger Cartesian component difference
when the target is distant. Local unit directions and surface bases do not carry the same distance
factor.

The full regression inputs and component residuals are included in the accompanying reproduction
materials.

\subsection{Compiler and runtime controls}

Five negative fixtures are compiled separately with \verb|cargo check --locked| and the pinned Rust
toolchain. They attempt quantity addition across dimensions, position subtraction across frames or
centers, a TT argument in the UT1 slot of \texttt{gmst\_iau2006}, and inversion of a lunar
observation using a Martian site. The control runner requires a nonzero exit status and the
expected \texttt{E0308} or \texttt{E0277} diagnostic for each fixture.

Runtime controls pair admissible inputs with nonfinite coordinates, invalid latitude, a
surface-normal singularity, zero or nonfinite directions, and mismatched observer values. Separate
upstream regressions test EOP sample endpoints, interior interpolation and rejection of
out-of-coverage and nonfinite queries. The fixtures, runtime assertions and recorded diagnostics
are included with the numerical records in \ref{sec:availability}.
