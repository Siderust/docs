\section{Applications in Observer Geometry}
\label{sec:application}

Surface observations provide two tests of the integrated APIs: an altitude-event search for a fixed
stellar direction from Earth, and a finite-distance Earth position viewed from the Moon. We define
their inputs and observables here; Section~7 specifies the independent reductions and Section~8
reports their residuals.

\subsection{Altitude-event search for Antares}
\label{sec:antares-example}

We evaluate Antares ($\alpha$ Scorpii) from a terrestrial site at geodetic latitude
$41.35403^\circ$ north and longitude $2.15017^\circ$ east, assuming zero WGS~84 ellipsoidal height.
The observation covers 1896 July 14 in the proleptic Gregorian calendar, with the day defined in
UT1. We seek the intervals during which the target's geometric altitude exceeds $15^\circ$.

Antares is represented by its SIMBAD ICRS coordinates at reference epoch J2000, $\alpha=16^{\mathrm
            h}29^{\mathrm m}24.45970^{\mathrm s}$ and $\delta=-26^\circ25'55.2094''$ \cite{simbadAntares}. The
calculation uses a fixed catalog direction, neglecting stellar proper motion and parallax. Siderust
transforms this direction into the true equatorial reference system of date before evaluating its
local horizontal coordinates.

The historical date exercises a UT1 observing day with TT derived from the prescribed $\Delta
    T=\mathrm{TT}-\mathrm{UT1}$ model. The implementation applies the public IAU 2006/2000A
bias--precession--nutation operation and then the true-of-date horizontal interface with explicit
TT and UT1. An \texttt{AltitudeProvider} adapter supplies this calculation to the event searches.
Section~\ref{sec:observation-method} gives the time prescription and search convention.

The event-search tools locate the $15^\circ$ crossings with a numerical time tolerance of
\ViewingEventTolerance{} s, yielding the interval \ViewingRefinedSpans{} UT1. Upper culmination
occurs at \ViewingCulminationTime{} UT1 with geometric altitude
$\ViewingCulminationAltitude^\circ$.

A separate five-minute sampling grid provides a complete view of the target's altitude evolution.
Including both endpoints of the day, the grid contains \ViewingSampleCount{} epochs, of which
\ViewingSelectedCount{} satisfy the strict altitude constraint. The selected samples span
\ViewingSpans{} UT1, with a maximum sampled altitude of $\ViewingMaximumAltitude^\circ$ at
\ViewingMaximumTime{} UT1.

Figure~\ref{fig:viewing-window} displays the altitude curve and enlarges the two threshold
crossings. The sampled grid brackets these crossings within \ViewingBrackets{} UT1, respectively,
while the event search provides refined crossing times. Both calculations use the same altitude
function. No solar-illumination or nighttime constraint is applied.

\subsection{Earth seen from a lunar surface site}
\label{sec:lunar-example}

Earth's finite distance makes observer displacement relevant to its direction in the lunar sky. The
calculation therefore uses an ephemeris position and includes the surface station's offset from the
Moon's center.

We consider a hypothetical lunar station at $20.17^\circ$ east longitude and $-1.10^\circ$
planetocentric latitude, at zero height above the Moon's reference sphere of radius $1737.4$ km.
The observation is evaluated at 2017 October 1, 17:14:00 TT. Our objective is to determine Earth's
geometric altitude, azimuth and range from this location.

The DE440s ephemeris supplies Earth's position relative to the lunar center. The lunar body model
supplies the reference surface and body-fixed orientation. The generic observation interface
transforms the finite target position into the observer's local horizon system.

The ephemeris-derived position has type \texttt{Position<Selenocentric, ICRS, Kilometer>}. The
\texttt{ICRS} marker represents the documented ICRF realization of DE440s's SPICE \texttt{J2000}
axes; no ecliptic transformation is introduced in this application. The epoch retains its TT scale,
with the required TT-to-TDB conversion applied for ephemeris evaluation and lunar orientation. The
helper uses the public SPK evaluator with explicit TDB seconds to retain the input epoch resolution
(Section~\ref{sec:observation-method}).

The lunar orientation follows the analytical \texttt{IAU\_MOON} model, exposed in Siderust as
\texttt{MoonIauMeanEarth}. It approximates the lunar mean-Earth/polar-axis convention and is
distinct from principal-axes and DE-specific binary-PCK frame realizations
\cite{archinal2011,naiflunarframes}. The spherical reference surface gives a radial local vertical.

The observation geometry can be expressed compactly. Let $\boldsymbol r$ denote Earth's
selenocentric inertial position, $R$ the inertial-to-body-fixed rotation and $\boldsymbol s$ the
observer's body-fixed position. The vector in the local north--east--up system is

\begin{equation}
    \boldsymbol\ell =
    \begin{pmatrix}
        \boldsymbol n^{\mathsf T} \\
        \boldsymbol e^{\mathsf T} \\
        \boldsymbol u^{\mathsf T}
    \end{pmatrix}
    (R\boldsymbol r-\boldsymbol s),
\end{equation}

where $\boldsymbol n$, $\boldsymbol e$ and $\boldsymbol u$ are the local north, east and up unit
vectors.

The observer-relative range is $\lVert\boldsymbol\ell\rVert$, while altitude and azimuth follow
from

\begin{align}
    h & =
    \operatorname{atan2}
    \left(
    \ell_u,
    \sqrt{\ell_n^2+\ell_e^2}
    \right), \\
    A & =
    \operatorname{atan2}(\ell_e,\ell_n)
    \pmod{2\pi}.
\end{align}

Azimuth is measured from north through east. The subtraction of the observer's position is
essential: it preserves the finite-distance geometry and allows the resulting direction to depend
on the actual surface location.

The resulting observation places Earth at a geometric altitude of $\EarthAltitude^\circ$ and an
azimuth of $\EarthAzimuth^\circ$, at a range of $\EarthRange$ km.

The importance of finite-distance representation becomes evident when the surface observation is
compared with the direction from the Moon's center. The corresponding center-based altitude is
$\CentreAltitude^\circ$, whereas the surface observer measures $\EarthAltitude^\circ$. The
difference, approximately $\EarthParallax^\circ$, arises from the displacement of the observer
relative to the lunar center.

The computed Earth position lies above the local horizon at the selected epoch. The observable is a
geometric pointing direction at a single epoch. Light-time corrections, aberration, gravitational
deflection and terrain masking are outside the evaluated model.
